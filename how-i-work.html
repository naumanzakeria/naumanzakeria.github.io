How I Work

The Playbooks I Use — and the Results They've Produced

Three frameworks built from 10+ years across Customer Success, Customer Experience, and Customer Service. Below them: real outcomes from putting these into practice.

Playbook 1

THE CUSTOMER SUCCESS PLAYBOOK

What this is: The framework I use to take a customer from signed contract to long-term, expanding partnership. Built and refined across ACE Union, TCP, and i2cinc — managing portfolios from £350K to $1.5M+ in ARR, and onboarding 15+ enterprise customers along the way.

Why it exists: A signed contract is a hypothesis, not a success. This playbook is how I test that hypothesis on purpose, instead of finding out the hard way at renewal.

Stage 1 — Onboarding: Confidence Over Speed

Most onboarding processes are built to get the customer "live" as fast as possible. I think that's the wrong target. Speed without understanding just moves the churn risk downstream instead of removing it. The real goal is confidence — a customer who knows *why* they're doing what they're doing, not just that they've technically finished a checklist.

In practice:

  • Before kickoff, I map the account's real stakeholder structure — who uses the product daily, who owns the budget, who takes the blame internally if it fails. Relying only on your day-to-day contact is how you lose an account to a reorg you never saw coming.
  • I build the success plan *with* the customer, not for them. A plan they helped write becomes a shared commitment. A plan you hand them becomes homework they'll ignore.
  • I check for understanding at each milestone, not just completion. "Did you finish the setup?" is a different question from "do you know why this setup matters for what you told me you wanted?"

What I watch for: A customer who's technically "live" but hasn't logged back in within the first week. That's not a slow start — that's a warning.

Stage 2 — Adoption: Usage Is the Only Honest Metric

Logo count and kickoff-call sentiment are vanity metrics. They measure whether a deal closed and whether a meeting felt pleasant — not whether the customer is actually getting value. Usage is the only number that doesn't lie.

In practice:

  • I track which specific features get opened in week two, not just whether onboarding was "completed." Everyone's polite in week one; the truth shows up in week three.
  • I tie every adoption push directly back to the outcome the customer told me they wanted in the success plan — not to whichever feature Product wants adopted this quarter.
  • I treat declining engagement as a prompt to reach out personally, not as something the health-score dashboard will eventually flag for me.

What I watch for: A previously active account that goes quiet without complaining. Silence is rarely peace. It's usually the sound of someone who's already decided.

Stage 3 — Health Monitoring: Signals Are Clues, Not Verdicts

CSAT, NPS, and engagement scores tell you *what* happened. They almost never tell you *why*. Treating them as verdicts instead of clues is how companies get blindsided by churn they technically had data on the whole time.

In practice:

  • A dropping metric is a prompt to ask a specific question, not a box to check off in a weekly report.
  • I separate effort from satisfaction — a customer can rate an interaction 5/5 after four transfers and three repeated explanations. That's not a win. That's a customer being polite on their way out the door.
  • I pay close attention to silence from previously vocal stakeholders in QBRs. No complaint isn't the same as no problem — sometimes it's the sound of someone who's stopped seeing the point in giving feedback.

What I watch for: Rising CSAT alongside falling engagement. That combination isn't a contradiction — it's a sign I'm measuring politeness instead of value.

Stage 4 — Renewal: Make It a Formality, Not a Negotiation

If I'm fighting for a renewal in the final 30 days, I've already lost the real battle — it happened months earlier, quietly, and I missed it.

In practice:

  • Value gets proven continuously throughout the relationship, never pitched retroactively in a QBR deck the week before renewal.
  • I treat every enterprise renewal like I'm winning the account for the first time. Champions leave. Budgets get reviewed by people who never sat in on the original sale. Good history doesn't renew itself automatically.
  • I report account health in the customer's language — protected revenue, time saved, risk avoided — not mine (feature adoption percentages an enterprise stakeholder's boss doesn't care about).

What I watch for: A renewal conversation that feels like a sales pitch instead of a formality. If it feels like selling, the relationship work happened too late.

Stage 5 — Escalation: Senior Work, Not Overflow

Complex account issues are not beneath a Customer Success leader — they're the job at its highest stakes, where trust gets permanently earned or permanently lost.

In practice:

  • I've personally acted as the senior escalation point for the most complicated account issues, coordinating Sales, Compliance, Product, Marketing, and Operations to remove roadblocks.
  • I communicate proactively, before the customer has to ask for a status update. A customer chasing you for news has already started losing confidence.
  • I treat a well-handled escalation as a trust deposit, not just a fire that's been put out. Customers remember how you handled the moment things went wrong far more than they remember when things went right.

What I watch for: Whether I'm being looped into an escalation early enough to actually shape the outcome, or only late enough to apologize for it.

Playbook 2

THE CUSTOMER EXPERIENCE PLAYBOOK

What this is: The framework I use to make sure a customer's experience is consistent — not just good in the specific moments a company happens to be paying attention to.

Why it exists: Customers don't experience a company as an org chart or a strategy deck. They experience it as a sequence of specific, easy-to-overlook moments — and most companies only actively manage the ones they've labeled "customer-facing," while the accidental ones quietly do the damage.

Principle 1 — Every Interaction Counts, Not Just the Ones With a Budget

A confusing invoice. An automated email sent at 11pm. One five-minute call. Customers generalize a company's entire character from a single unglamorous moment, and that's not unfair — it's just how people actually process trust.

In practice:

  • I audit the *unowned* interactions — the ones no single department has explicitly claimed responsibility for, like billing communications or system-generated emails.
  • I hold every customer-facing moment to the same standard, whether or not it has a project budget or an executive sponsor attached to it.
  • I ask, for any new process: "if a customer only ever saw this one moment, what would they conclude about us?"

Principle 2 — Map the Journey the Customer Actually Takes

Not the journey in your org chart — departments rarely experience their own handoffs as friction. The customer does.

In practice:

  • I walk the full journey myself, onboarding through renewal, as if I were the customer receiving it cold.
  • I identify every handoff point specifically, because handoffs are almost always where trust quietly leaks — the second person on a call rarely knows what the first person already promised.
  • I fix the moments that force a customer to repeat themselves. Few things say "nobody here is talking to each other" faster than asking a customer the same question twice.

Principle 3 — Measure Beyond CSAT

CSAT measures a moment. It was never built to measure a relationship, and treating it as the whole picture hides real problems behind a satisfying-looking average.

In practice:

  • I track effort: how many times did the customer have to explain their problem before it actually got solved?
  • I track recurrence: is the same underlying issue coming back from the same account? A closed ticket isn't the same thing as a solved problem.
  • I track silence: a previously vocal stakeholder going quiet is frequently a stronger churn signal than an active complaint.
  • I track outcome over interaction: did the customer actually accomplish what they came to do, or did they just receive a polite, on-time response?

Principle 4 — Turn Complaints Into Input, Not Just Tickets

A complaint is free, unusually honest market research from a customer who cared enough to tell you instead of just quietly leaving — which is what most unhappy customers actually do.

In practice:

  • I route recurring complaint themes directly into product and process conversations, not just into a support-volume report nobody outside the team reads.
  • I close the loop visibly. A customer whose serious complaint led to a real, visible fix often becomes more loyal than a customer who never had a problem — they've now seen what happens under pressure, not just what the marketing promises.
  • I separate "the ticket closed fast" from "the underlying issue got fixed." Response-time metrics reward the first. Only genuine follow-through delivers the second.

Principle 5 — Service Recovery Is a Skill, Not a Script

When something goes wrong, how you recover matters more to the relationship than the failure itself ever did.

In practice:

  • I acknowledge the real issue plainly, and never hide behind "policy" as a wall between me and an honest answer.
  • When I genuinely can't give a customer what they're asking for, I explain the actual constraint instead of deflecting — trust is built by *how* you say no, not by avoiding saying it.
  • I follow through on every commitment down to the letter, because a broken small promise reads to a customer as a signal that nothing else about the relationship is safe either.
Playbook 3

THE CUSTOMER SERVICE PLAYBOOK

What this is: The framework built from years of direct, front-line customer service — including supporting FinTech customers through real transaction and account issues, under real financial regulations, where the stakes were the customer's actual money.

Why it exists: Customer Service is where trust gets built or lost fastest, in real time, with no time to prepare a strategy deck first.

Step 1 — Hear the Real Problem, Not Just the Stated One

Most customers don't lead with their actual issue. "The reporting feature is confusing" often really means "I don't trust the numbers I have to present to my own boss." Solve the first sentence and you'll miss the second one entirely.

In practice:

  • I ask one genuine clarifying question before offering a solution, even when the "obvious" answer seems ready to go.
  • I listen for the emotion underneath the words — anxious, embarrassed, frustrated — because it changes how the response needs to be delivered, not just what the response contains.

Step 2 — Resolve With Ownership, Not Just Escalation

Customers don't actually want to be transferred. They want someone to own their problem, start to finish.

In practice:

  • I stay the customer's point of contact even when the ultimate fix requires pulling in another team — I don't disappear the moment it's technically "someone else's ticket."
  • I minimize how many times a customer has to re-explain their situation. Every repeat conversation quietly erodes trust, even when each individual agent is being perfectly polite.
  • I close the loop personally where possible, rather than letting a resolved ticket close silently with no human follow-up.

Step 3 — Operate Within Real Constraints, Honestly

In regulated environments especially — I spent years supporting FinTech customers under real financial regulations — you can't always give the customer exactly what they're asking for. You can always explain why, clearly and respectfully.

In practice:

  • I never hide behind "policy" as a wall. I explain the actual constraint underneath it, in plain language.
  • I hold two things at once: making the customer feel genuinely heard, and staying strictly within regulatory or company boundaries that don't bend for a frustrated request.
  • I've learned that customers forgive a "no" far more easily than they forgive a vague or evasive one.

Step 4 — Multi-Channel Consistency

A customer who emails, then calls, then chats should never feel like they're starting over each time.

In practice:

  • I keep context visible and available across channels — phone, email, chat — so a customer isn't forced to repeat their situation to a third person after already explaining it twice.
  • I match tone to the channel (a chat reply reads differently than a phone call) without ever changing the substance of the actual answer.

Step 5 — Treat Operational Discipline as Part of Service

Great service isn't only the individual interaction — it's built on invisible infrastructure the customer never sees but always feels.

In practice:

  • Staffing, scheduling, and demand forecasting directly shape how long a customer waits and how much bandwidth an agent has to actually listen, not just move fast. Early in my career, reducing scheduling conflicts by 20% through demand analysis was, underneath the operations language, a direct customer experience improvement.
  • When a service issue keeps recurring, I check for an operational root cause — a staffing gap, a process bottleneck — before assuming it's simply a training problem with the person on the phone.
