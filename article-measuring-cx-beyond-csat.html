Measuring Customer Experience Beyond CSAT

Nauman Zakeria · Customer Success, Experience & Service Leader

I've used CSAT scores throughout my career, and I'll say something that might sound strange coming from someone who's improved CSAT by 15% more than once: CSAT alone is one of the most misleading numbers in Customer Success if it's the only thing you're watching.

Here's the problem. CSAT measures a moment, not a relationship. A customer can rate a single support interaction 5 out of 5 — the agent was friendly, the ticket got closed fast — and still be quietly planning to churn, because the underlying reason they contacted support in the first place keeps happening. You can have rising CSAT and falling retention at the same time. I've seen it. It's not a contradiction; it's a sign you're measuring politeness instead of value.

What I've learned to watch alongside CSAT:

Effort, not just satisfaction. How many times did the customer have to explain their problem before it got solved? A high CSAT score after four transfers and three repeated explanations is a customer being polite on the way out the door.

Recurrence. Is the same customer contacting support about the same underlying issue repeatedly? A resolved ticket isn't the same as a solved problem. If it keeps coming back, CSAT is scoring the bandage, not the wound.

Silence. Some of the most dangerous accounts are the ones that stop rating anything at all — the previously vocal stakeholder who's gone quiet in QBRs. No complaint isn't the same as no problem. Sometimes it's the sound of someone who's already decided not to renew and no longer sees the point in giving feedback.

Outcome, not interaction. Did the customer actually accomplish what they came to do, or did they just get a polite response? I've found the two get confused constantly, especially when teams are incentivized on response time and satisfaction ratings rather than resolution quality.

CSAT is a useful signal. It was never meant to be the whole picture, and treating it that way lets real problems hide behind a satisfying-sounding average.
