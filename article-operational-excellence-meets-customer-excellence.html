Operational Excellence Meets Customer Excellence

Nauman Zakeria · Customer Success, Experience & Service Leader

Early in my career, as a Workforce Analyst, I reduced scheduling conflicts by 20% through demand and call-volume analysis. On paper, that's an operations story — spreadsheets, forecasting, staffing models. In practice, it was a customer experience story wearing an operations costume, and that's a connection a lot of companies never quite make.

Here's why it mattered: every scheduling conflict I eliminated was a customer who didn't wait on hold an extra four minutes. Every improved forecast was a support team that wasn't understaffed during a predictable volume spike, scrambling and short-tempered by 2pm. The customer never saw my spreadsheet. They just experienced a shorter wait and a calmer agent — and had no idea that "calmer agent" was the direct downstream result of an operational decision made weeks earlier.

This is the connection I think gets lost when companies separate "operations" and "customer experience" into different departments with different KPIs and, often, different leadership entirely. Operational excellence isn't a back-office concern that's separate from customer excellence. It's the invisible infrastructure customer excellence is built on top of. A brilliant, empathetic support agent operating inside a badly staffed shift, working from bad data, will still deliver a worse customer experience than an average agent operating inside a well-run one.

I've carried that lesson forward into every Customer Success role since. When I look at an account that's struggling, I don't only ask "is the relationship being managed well?" I also ask "is there an operational gap — a process, a staffing pattern, a data blind spot — quietly making that relationship harder than it needs to be?" Often, the honest answer is yes, and no amount of relationship-building charm fixes a structural problem underneath it.

Companies that treat operations and customer experience as separate disciplines, measured by separate teams, are missing that one is almost always the hidden cause of the other. I've spent a career on both sides of that line, and I no longer see it as a line at all.
