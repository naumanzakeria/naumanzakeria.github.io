What 10+ Years in Customer Success Has Taught Me

Nauman Zakeria · Customer Success, Experience & Service Leader

Ten years ago, I was a Customer Services Specialist, on the phone with FinTech customers navigating real financial regulations with real anxiety about their own money. Today, I've led enterprise portfolios worth over $1.5M in ARR, mentored a team of Customer Success Advisors, and been named Employee of the Year. If you'd told me at the start that the through-line connecting those two points would be the exact same skill, I'm not sure I would have believed you. But it is.

The skill is this: actually listening to what a customer is telling you, underneath what they're saying.

Early on, I thought Customer Success was about having good answers. Ten years in, I know it's about asking better questions, because most customers don't lead with their real problem. The account that says "the reporting feature is confusing" is often really saying "I don't trust the numbers I'm being asked to present to my own boss." Learn to hear the second sentence, and you solve problems the first sentence never would have led you to.

I've also learned that the flashiest parts of this job — the big enterprise wins, the recognition — are not actually where the career gets built. The career gets built in the unglamorous consistency: the follow-up email you send even though the call already "went well," the account you keep checking on even after the health score turned green, the escalation you personally own instead of quietly routing to someone else's queue. Nobody claps for that. It's also the entire reason certain customers stayed for years instead of one renewal cycle.

Maybe the biggest lesson: the title changes — Customer Services Specialist, Workforce Analyst, Customer Success Analyst, Team Lead — but the actual job has never changed underneath it. It was never about the ticket, the dashboard, or the ARR number on the account. It was always about whether the person on the other end believed someone genuinely had their back. Ten years in, that's still the whole job. Everything else is just the tools we use to do it.
